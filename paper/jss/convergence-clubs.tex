\title{\pkg{convergence-clubs}: Phillips--Sul Club Convergence Analysis in \proglang{Python}}
\Plaintitle{convergence-clubs: Phillips-Sul Club Convergence Analysis in Python}
\Shorttitle{\pkg{convergence-clubs}}

\Abstract{
  Countries and regions often converge towards different paths rather than a common one,
  and the log $t$ test and clustering algorithm of Phillips and Sul identify these
  convergence clubs. The method is available in \proglang{R} as \pkg{ConvergenceClubs} and
  in \proglang{Stata} as \pkg{psecta}, but not in \proglang{Python}. \pkg{convergence-clubs}
  implements it with \pkg{numpy} and \pkg{pandas}, reproduces the \proglang{R} results to
  within $10^{-8}$, and reproduces the \proglang{Stata} classifications of eight published
  panels. Because the clubs the algorithm finds depend on several settings, the package also
  quantifies that dependence, through sensitivity analysis, bootstrap confidence in each
  membership and clubs estimated over rolling windows. This article describes the method,
  the design of the package and the checks of its correctness, and illustrates its use on
  a panel of GDP for 152 countries.
}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

Whether poor economies catch up with rich ones is among the oldest questions in growth
empirics. The log $t$ test of \citet{PhillipsSul2007} replaces the assumption of a single
common growth path with a model in which each unit has a transition path to its own
long-run level, and tests whether the cross-sectional dispersion of those paths shrinks.
When the test rejects convergence for the whole panel, a clustering algorithm
\citep{PhillipsSul2009} partitions the units into \emph{convergence clubs}, each of which
passes the test. The procedure is used in applied work on income, productivity, energy and
research spending \citep[for example][]{BanyulsVides2025}.

Two implementations are widely used: the \proglang{R} package \pkg{ConvergenceClubs}
\citep{SicheraPizzuto2019} and the \proglang{Stata} package \pkg{psecta}
\citep{Du2017}. They differ in choices the original papers leave open, so that a
classification depends on which one produced it. A researcher working in \proglang{Python}
has had to leave the language or reimplement the method.

This article presents \pkg{convergence-clubs}, a \proglang{Python} package with three aims.
First, to implement the method so that its results match both existing implementations,
with the differences between them explicit and selectable. Second, to treat the number
and membership of clubs as an estimate with uncertainty, since they change with the
settings. Third, to place the clubs in context with the classical convergence measures.
Section~\ref{sec:method} summarises the method and the choices on which the
implementations differ, Section~\ref{sec:design} describes the package,
Section~\ref{sec:classical} and Section~\ref{sec:robustness} describe the measures and the
robustness tools that accompany the clubs, Section~\ref{sec:validation} reports the
validation, and Section~\ref{sec:illustration} illustrates a complete analysis.

\section{The method} \label{sec:method}

\subsection{The log $t$ test}

Let $X_{it}$ be the (usually log and trend-filtered) observation of unit $i$ at time $t$,
for $i = 1, \dots, N$ and $t = 1, \dots, T$. The relative transition path is
$h_{it} = X_{it} / (N^{-1} \sum_i X_{it})$ and the cross-sectional variation is
$H_t = N^{-1} \sum_i (h_{it} - 1)^2$. Under convergence $H_t$ falls over time, and the
log $t$ regression
\begin{equation}
  \log \frac{H_1}{H_t} - 2 \log \log t = a + b \log t + u_t, \qquad t = \lfloor rT \rfloor, \dots, T,
\end{equation}
gives the test of the null $b \ge 0$ against divergence, rejected when the $t$-statistic
of $\hat{b}$ is below $-1.65$. The trimming $r$ discards the first periods. The regression
uses a heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC) covariance, with the
fixed-bandwidth (FQSB) or the \citet{Andrews1991} data-driven (AQSB) quadratic spectral
estimator.

\subsection{Clustering and merging}

The clustering sorts units by their final observations, forms a core group of top-ranked
units chosen by the log $t$ statistic, adds units one at a time while the club keeps
passing the test (subject to the threshold $c^*$), and repeats on the remaining units. Units that
cannot form a club are divergent. Clubs that converge together are then merged, with the
rules of \citet{PhillipsSul2009} (PS) or \citet{vonLynckerThoennissen2017} (vLT), and
divergent units can then be assigned to clubs by the same tests.

\subsection{Where the implementations differ} \label{sec:differences}

The papers leave several choices open, and the two implementations settle them
differently. Reading the sources of both gives the differences in Table~\ref{tab:diff}.

  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{p{3.2cm}p{5.2cm}p{5.2cm}}
    \toprule
    & \proglang{R} (\pkg{ConvergenceClubs}) & \proglang{Stata} (\pkg{psecta}) \\
    \midrule

None of them is wrong, but they can give different clubs on the same data. The package
implements both sets and lets them be mixed, so that a difference between two published
classifications can be traced to the rule that causes it
(Section~\ref{sec:validation}).

\section{The package} \label{sec:design}

\subsection{Design}

The package needs only \pkg{numpy} and \pkg{pandas}; plots, interactive charts and maps
are optional extras installed with \code{pip install "convergence-clubs[plot]"}. Its
functions follow those of the \proglang{R} package, so \proglang{R} code translates
directly, with \proglang{Python} names (\code{findClubs} becomes \code{find_clubs},
\code{estimateMod} becomes \code{log_t_test}). Results are immutable
objects that record the settings used, so that a table or a figure can be traced back to
the call that produced it. Clubs are indexed by position or by name, iterating over a
result gives its clubs, and every table is a \pkg{pandas} data frame.

The input is a wide data frame with one row per unit and one column per period.
\code{wide_panel} converts a long table, \code{describe_missing}, \code{fill_gaps} and
\code{balance} deal with missing values, and \code{hp_filter} applies the
\citet{Hodrick1997} filter when the data are not already filtered. Two panels are bundled:
GDP per capita for 152 countries and labour productivity for the samples of
\citet{Mendez2020}.

\subsection{An analysis in a few lines}

\begin{CodeChunk}

\end{CodeInput}

\begin{CodeInput}
>>> merged = clubs.merge("PS")      # club4 and club5 converge together
>>> merged.memberships()            # unit -> club
\end{CodeInput}
\end{CodeChunk}

Choices are arguments: \code{time_trim}, \code{hac_method}, \code{cstar},
\code{cstar_method}, \code{core_rule}, \code{sort_fraction} and the merging method.
\code{convention="Stata"} sets the \pkg{psecta} defaults of Table~\ref{tab:diff} at once,
and an explicit argument overrides the convention. A warning is issued, and
\code{converges} is false, for a club that fails its own test. \code{clubs.rename()} gives
clubs meaningful names in every table, plot and report.

Besides the library there is a command line, \code{convergence-clubs}, and
\code{cc.report(...)} writes the whole analysis (clubs, tests, robustness, classical
measures, figures) to a single HTML file.

\section{Placing the clubs in context} \label{sec:classical} 

Clubs describe where a panel splits. The package adds the classical measures that say how
the split relates to other notions of convergence, computed on the same clubs.

\textbf{$\beta$-convergence} \citep{Barro1992} regresses average growth on the initial log
level, with HC1 standard errors by default; a negative coefficient means convergence, with
speed and half-life derived from it. On the GDP panel there is no absolute convergence
($\hat\beta = -0.0004$, $p = 0.81$), but conditional on the club ($\hat\beta = -0.0151$,
$t = -14.8$) there is, with a half-life of 33 years. This is the pattern clubs describe:
convergence within groups and not across them.

\textbf{$\sigma$-convergence} tests whether the dispersion falls over time, with a
Newey--West trend test. The dispersion of all 152 countries rises by 16\% between 1970 and
2003, while it falls significantly within each of the five clubs with at least three
countries (Figure~\ref{fig:sigma}).

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{sigma.pdf}
  \caption{Dispersion of log GDP per capita, indexed to its first-year value, for all
    countries and by club.}
  \label{fig:sigma}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Distribution dynamics} \citep{Quah1993,Quah1996} look at the shape of the
distribution rather than its dispersion. The package estimates the transition matrix of
income relative to the cross-sectional mean over five-year lags, and the ergodic distribution it implies.
On the GDP panel, 28\% of countries end up in the poorest class and 32\% in the richest,
against 12\% in the middle one, the ``twin peaks'' that clubs correspond to
(Figure~\ref{fig:dynamics}).

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{dynamics.pdf}
  \caption{Transition probabilities between five classes of relative income over five years,
    and the initial, final and ergodic distribution of the classes.}
  \label{fig:dynamics}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Pairwise convergence} \citep{Pesaran2007} tests whether the gap between every pair
of units is stationary, with an augmented Dickey--Fuller test, and optionally confirms it
with the KPSS test. With clubs, the pairs split into within-club and between-club pairs,
which checks the clustering independently. On the GDP panel the unit root is rejected for
12.8\% of the 11\,476 pairs, for 15.9\% of the 2\,629 pairs within clubs and 11.8\% of the
8\,847 pairs between them. The ordering is the one the clubs predict, and the shares are
low because 34 annual observations give the test little power.

An ordered logit of club membership on covariates completes the set, to ask what explains
which club a country is in.

\section{How robust are the clubs?} \label{sec:robustness}

The clubs the algorithm finds depend on the settings and on the sample, and published
classifications rarely report by how much. The package provides three tools.

\code{sensitivity()} re-estimates the clubs over the product of lists of settings
(trimming, HAC method, $c^*$, merging, sorting) and reports, for each run, the number and
sizes of clubs and its agreement with the baseline run through the adjusted Rand index,
and for each unit the share of runs in which it stays with its baseline club mates
(stability).

\code{bootstrap()} perturbs the data instead of the settings, with three resampling
schemes: random 80\% subsets of the units, a block bootstrap of the business-cycle
component (which needs the unfiltered data and filters again), and removal of the last
periods, where the HP filter is least reliable. Periods are never resampled independently,
because the log $t$ regression depends on the time index. The result gives, for each unit,
the share of resamples in which it keeps its club mates, and \code{uncertain(0.8)} lists
those below a threshold.

\code{rolling_clubs()} estimates the clubs in windows of periods and follows the units
between windows, with the units that leave their club mates and an alluvial plot of the
flows.

Analyses with many re-estimations run in parallel with \code{n_jobs}, and the result does
not depend on the number of workers; the test suite checks this. The lag search of the
augmented Dickey--Fuller test, which dominates the pairwise test, uses a single QR
decomposition for all candidate lags instead of one regression for each. Table~\ref{tab:time}
gives timings on a synthetic panel.

\section{Validation} \label{sec:validation}

\textbf{Against \proglang{R}.} Test statistics, club sizes and memberships are compared
with fixtures generated by \pkg{ConvergenceClubs} 2.2.5. They cover the log $t$ test on
several subsets, clustering under six configurations (both HAC methods, trimming, fixed
and incremental $c^*$) and merging with both rules, on the GDP panel and three simulated
panels with divergent units and with $c^*$ reaching its cap. Memberships match exactly and
the statistics to a relative tolerance of $10^{-8}$. The comparison is repeated weekly
against the current release of the \proglang{R} package. The GDP example reproduces the
published table of \citet{SicheraPizzuto2019}.

\textbf{Against \proglang{Stata}.} \citet{Mendez2020} reports the \pkg{psecta} and
\pkg{imergeclub} classifications of four variables (labour productivity, the
capital--output ratio, human capital and total factor productivity) in two samples (26
high-income and 82 other countries). With \code{convention="Stata"} the package
reproduces all eight classifications, both before and after merging. Under the
\proglang{R} conventions six of the eight are reproduced. Each of the other two is
explained by one of the rules of Table~\ref{tab:diff}: the core group in one, and in the
other the need to raise $c^*$ to 4.85, above the cap of 3 of the \proglang{R} package.

\textbf{Known differences.} Two inconsistencies were found in \pkg{ConvergenceClubs}
2.2.5 and reported upstream with a fix. When $c^*$ exceeds its cap, the club falls back to
its core group, and \proglang{R} reports the regression of the core plus the rejected
candidates, whereas the package reports that of the core; memberships are the same. The
second merging test of the vLT rule ignores the HAC method in \proglang{R}, always using
FQSB; the package follows the fix. When the Andrews bandwidth is zero the \proglang{R}
dependency \pkg{sandwich} raises an error, and the package falls back to White's
estimator. The documentation lists these differences.

\textbf{Other tests.} The classical measures are compared with \pkg{statsmodels} and
\pkg{scipy}: the $\beta$ regression, the Newey--West trend, kernel densities, and the ADF and
KPSS tests (the latter on 360 and 1\,800 simulated series). The suite has 446 tests with
full line coverage, and continuous integration runs it on the oldest and newest supported
versions of the dependencies. All numbers in this article are reproduced by
\code{paper/jss/replication.py} in the repository, which stops if any of them differs.

\section{Illustration} \label{sec:illustration}

\subsection{The clubs}

On the GDP panel (152 countries, 1970--2003, log and HP-filtered with $\lambda = 400$)
the whole sample diverges: the log $t$ statistic is $-159.6$. The algorithm finds seven
clubs of 50, 30, 21, 24, 14, 11 and 2 countries, each passing the test, and the PS rule
merges the fourth and fifth, leaving six. Figure~\ref{fig:paths} shows the relative
transition paths of the six merged clubs. The clubs separate in the way the method
predicts: the paths of the richest clubs drift up and stay flat, and those of the poorest
decline.

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{transition_paths.pdf}
  \caption{Relative transition paths $h_{it}$ of the six clubs after PS merging, with the
    club average in colour and the last panel comparing the averages.}
  \label{fig:paths}
\end{figure}

\subsection{How firm are they?}

The number of clubs is not a fixed fact of the data. Over sixteen combinations of
trimming (four values), HAC method (two) and merging (none or PS), the package finds
between four and nine clubs. For each country, the share of runs in which it stays with
its baseline club mates has a median of 0.84 and a minimum of 0.41
(\code{cc.sensitivity(...).stability()}).

The bootstrap gives a second view. In 200 resamples of 80\% of the units
(\code{cc.bootstrap(data, 200, merge="PS", seed=0)}), 52 of the 152 countries stay with
their club mates in fewer than 80\% of the runs. Figure~\ref{fig:stability} shows
the share for each country by club.

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{bootstrap_stability.pdf}
  \caption{Share of 200 bootstrap resamples in which each country stays with its
    baseline club mates, by club. The line marks 0.8.}
  \label{fig:stability}
\end{figure}

Reporting this is the point of these tools. Two analyses with the same data and
different but defensible settings can report different numbers of clubs, and the
published classifications rarely say how much of the result survives. The same method
was used for research spending in the European Union \citep{BanyulsVides2025}.

\section{Summary} \label{sec:summary}

\pkg{convergence-clubs} makes the Phillips--Sul method available in \proglang{Python},
reproduces both existing implementations on published results, makes the points where they
differ explicit, and adds the tools to judge how robust the clubs are and how they relate to
other notions of convergence. The package is on PyPI and the source and documentation are
at \url{https://github.com/bbanyuls/club-convergence}. Possible extensions are other
covariance estimators in the log $t$ regression and a comparison of the clubs with
alternative clustering methods.
